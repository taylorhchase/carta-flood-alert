Subject: A concrete, data-backed look at tidal flooding on CARTA's most vulnerable routes

Hi [CARTA contact name],

I've been working on a prototype tool that gives CARTA riders advance
warning of tidal flooding disruptions — the night before, not during —
and I wanted to reach out now that it has real, testable findings
behind it rather than just a concept.

The core idea: Charleston's tides are predictable 48 hours in advance,
but the connection between that public NOAA data and CARTA's actual
route network has never been built. Riders currently find out a route
is disrupted only after they're already at the stop.

Using CARTA's public GTFS feed and USGS elevation data, I mapped
ground elevation at all 155 stops across seven of your highest-traffic
and most flood-exposed routes (DASH 213, DASH 211, DASH 210, Route 20,
Route 203, Route 30, and XP1), then checked each stop against NOAA's
published minor/moderate/major coastal flood thresholds for Charleston
Harbor (Station 8665530). A few findings that I think would be useful
to your operations team:

  - Route 213 (Lockwood/Calhoun DASH) is the most exposed route
    reviewed: 58% of its stops sit low enough to flood at minor flood
    stage — a level Charleston Harbor reaches roughly 25 times a year.
  - Route 203 (Medical Shuttle) has the highest percentage of any
    route (71% of its 7 stops), concentrated in the MUSC medical
    district corridor your own weather policy already flags as a
    priority to keep serving.
  - Route 20 (King Street/Meeting) is the least exposed by far — only
    4% of stops at risk, and only at the rarest (major) flood stage,
    about 3 times a year.

I want to be upfront about what this is and isn't: it's a screening
model built from elevation and public tide data, not a validated
hydrological model — it doesn't yet account for drainage capacity or
storm surge. But it's a real, reproducible starting point, and I'd
like to validate it against the City's road closure logs and FEMA's
flood maps as a next step.

What I'm hoping for is a short conversation — 20-30 minutes — with
whoever on your team thinks about weather-related service disruptions,
to hear what would actually be useful to you: which routes to
prioritize, how far in advance a warning needs to arrive to be
actionable, and whether a tool like this could plug into anything
you're already doing.

I can send over the full stop-by-stop and route-by-route data, along
with a plain-language summary, ahead of any call.

Thank you for your time and for the work you do keeping Charleston
moving — I'd love the chance to make it a little more predictable for
your riders on flood days.
